Winning the iQOO Battle

The client

What iQOO is actually paying ₹40 lakh for

Read the rubric as a purchase order and it becomes obvious. Twenty-five percent of your score is read off device telemetry, not self-reported — they built a tracker to measure how hard you used the phone. They force you to build on the phone during Red Light. They gave Office Kit, a feature that shipped weeks ago with zero mindshare, its own 10% line. None of that is about your idea. It is about their device.

The context that explains it: the iQOO 15 launched in India at ₹72,999–79,999. That is Samsung and OnePlus flagship money for a brand that has spent five years being known as the cheap fast one for gamers. Their hardware argument is already won — Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5, three 50MP cameras, a 7,000mAh cell, a 14,000mm² vapour chamber. What they do not have is a software reason to cost ₹73,000, and they have no developer ecosystem at all. Apple has WWDC. Samsung has SDC. iQOO has this hackathon.

So the question every judge is silently scoring is not “is this a good product.” It is: could this have been built on a ₹15,000 phone? If yes, you are invisible, no matter how polished.

The precedent

Why Tokito and Anchor won

Two very different projects. Strip them down and they are the same shape.

Tokito Companion — 1st, Working Professionals

Point the phone at a circuit board; on-device vision, voice and AR bring the project’s schematic context onto the physical board in front of you. Why it won:

  • It is physically impossible without a phone. You cannot demo it on a laptop. Camera → understanding → overlay on the real world. The device isn’t a container for the product, it is the product.
  • Four device surfaces at once — camera, NPU, voice, display-as-AR. That is close to the ceiling of the 15% “creative phone use” line by construction.
  • The poster’s own highlight was the hardware: “the craziest part was seeing all of this running locally on an iQOO 15.” That sentence is what iQOO paid for.
  • It demos in ten seconds with no explanation. Point, see, understand.

Anchor — 1st Runner-Up, ₹80,000

Detects GPS spoofing by checking whether a reported location is physically plausible against movement and device signals. Why it won:

  • Sensor fusion is the entire product. Pure phone-as-instrument.
  • They built the app on the phone — Termux, proot, Office Kit, Codex — and made that part of the story. Maximum HackTracker and Office Kit telemetry, plus a narrative iQOO loves.
  • They opened with a scenario, not a feature list. The stolen-saree delivery story. Their own stated takeaway: “A good project isn’t enough. People need to understand why they should care.”
  • They pre-researched the failure modes — battery, memory, accuracy, latency, what happens when it’s wrong — because judges ask, and having the answer reads as engineering maturity.
  • They pre-empted the AI question: “we designed the architecture ourselves, then used Codex to implement.”

The shared pattern: a sensor → inference → real-world-consequence loop that only a phone can close, demoed in under fifteen seconds, wrapped in a story that made the problem feel physical.

Note what neither of them had: a published research paper, a validated market, or six months of prior work. You have all three. That is your edge — but only after you convert it into a device-first product, which is what the rest of this document is about.

Build it. The domain is unglamorous but the mechanics are near-perfect for this rubric: continuous multimodal inference, two devices in different roles, offline by necessity, real market, real stakes, and a research story no other team can tell. The risk is not the idea — it is that the current spec casts the iQOO in the wrong role.

The one thing in your spec that would cost you the win

Your build spec assigns the old phone from a drawer to be the Guardian — the device at the crib that runs all the vision, all the audio and all the inference — and gives the iQOO the parent unit, where it displays a video feed and some cards.

Read that back as an iQOO marketing lead would. You are demonstrating, on their stage, with their loaner device, that a five-year-old phone does the AI and the flagship is the dumb screen. It is the most expensive possible framing error, and it is a fifteen-minute fix.

Swap the roles. The iQOO is the Guardian. Put the flagship at the crib doing ten hours of continuous vision + audio + LLM inference, and let the second phone be the viewport that could be anything.

Now every hardware fact you were going to bury becomes an argument: the vapour chamber is why it doesn’t thermally throttle at hour six; the 7,000mAh cell is why it lasts the night; the periscope telephoto is why it can sit two metres away instead of hovering over the baby; the NPU is why three models run at once without the phone becoming a hand-warmer. The pitch line writes itself: “the specs you bought for eight hours of BGMI are exactly the specs that let this watch a crib all night.”

The reframe

Stop building a monitor. Build the loop that closes.

Every product in this category — Nanit, Cubo AI, Miku, and your current spec — ends at notify. Something happens, a parent is woken, the parent fixes it. That is where all of them stop, and it is where the interesting half of the problem starts.

The single highest-value addition to Nila is an action tier between detection and waking the parent. Most night wakings self-settle. Some need a sound. Some need the room to be cooler. Only a few need a human. Right now the product treats all four identically.

Office Kit — give the laptop a job the phone can’t do

Mirroring the event timeline, as your spec currently proposes, is a weak use — it is the phone’s screen on a bigger screen, and a judge will read it as a checkbox. Make the laptop the night review station instead: scrub a full night’s timeline with the intensity curves, mark false alarms to tune the thresholds, and export a one-page PDF for the paediatric visit. That is a genuinely desktop-shaped task, you will actually use it while debugging from hour 3, and it produces an artifact you can hand a judge.

The health question

What you can measure without becoming a medical device

You asked about health monitoring. The answer has a hard line through it, and knowing exactly where that line sits is itself worth points — it reads as maturity, and one informed judge is enough to end a pitch that gets it wrong.

The precedent is Owlet. In October 2021 the FDA sent them a warning letter over the Smart Sock: the device carried a disclaimer, and the FDA held that alerting caregivers to low oxygen saturation made it an unapproved medical device regardless. Owlet pulled it from the US market and only returned two years later with de novo clearance. Anything that alerts on a physiological state is a medical device. That rules out breathing rate, apnoea alerts, heart rate, and oxygen — including via camera, including with a disclaimer.

Don’t
Breathing, vitals, apnoea, SIDS, “sleep safety score”

Even framed as “stillness detection,” an alert on the absence of breathing motion is an apnoea alarm. Do not build it, do not show it, do not put it on a roadmap slide.

Don’t
Pathology screening from cry acoustics

Your 8-class merged model with the asphyxia and hearing-loss labels is scientifically interesting and clinically radioactive. Keep it in the research slide as prior work; keep it out of the app entirely.

Do
Objective cry-duration tracking against the colic criteria

This is the best health feature available to you, and it is genuinely novel. Infantile colic is defined by the Wessel rule of threes — crying ≥3 hours a day, ≥3 days a week, for ≥3 weeks — carried into the Rome IV criteria for research use. It is purely a measurement of duration. No diagnosis, no physiology.

Here is why that last one is strong enough to lead a slide. The Rome IV committee’s own stated objection to the Wessel criteria is that they are caregiver-reported — “arbitrary, culturally dependent, impractical.” Parents currently track this with a paper diary while sleep-deprived. You are proposing an instrument that measures the exact quantity the clinical standard is built on, objectively, automatically, on-device, and exports it as a page a paediatrician can read.

“We don’t diagnose anything. We measure the one number the clinical definition is actually made of, and we measure it better than a sleep-deprived parent with a notebook.”

That claim survives any level of scrutiny, gives Office Kit a real reason to exist, and turns your cry work into a health contribution without going anywhere near regulated territory. Safe companions to it: sleep-window logs, feed and change intervals, and nursery light levels — all measured, none interpreted.

The pitch

Five minutes, rewritten

Your existing demo script opens with a self-test and a latency number. That is a good engineering opener and a weak story opener. Anchor’s lesson was that the room has to feel the problem before it can care about the solution — and Tokito’s was that the phone should visibly do something impossible before you have said a word.

0:00
Cold open. Say nothing.

The iQOO is on the table facing a doll. Play a recorded cry from a small speaker. The phone detects it, shows its reasoning on screen, plays a shushing sound back — and then the air conditioner in the room turns on. Let that land for two full seconds before you speak.

“Nobody woke up. That’s the whole product.”
0:40
The stake. One slide, three numbers.

From your own deck, all sourced: 5.6% of parents of six-month-olds reported smothering, slapping or shaking their baby over crying. 27% report problems with infant crying in the first four months. ~20% of infants cry excessively, linked to parental anxiety and depression.

“The existing answer costs ₹30,000, needs your Wi-Fi, and uploads video of your baby.”
1:20
Safety, live, with the doll.

Roll it prone — L3 fires. Towel over the face — L3. Lift it toward the rail — L4, and the crib phone alarms on its own.

“That alarm never touched the network. It has to beat the fall, not report it.”
2:20
The honesty beat. This is the moment they remember.

Show the Reading card with its evidence chips and its hedge line. Then the research: published cry classifiers report 96–99%. You built one — 98.76%. Then you split by infant instead of by clip and it fell to chance, matching the 2026 controlled study on this corpus.

“So we don’t tell you why your baby is crying. We show you the evidence and we say what we can’t know.”
3:10
Why it needs this phone.

Airplane mode on both devices — everything still works. Then the numbers: the latency table, NPU vs GPU vs CPU. The endurance run: hours watched, battery drained, peak surface temperature. Gemma 3n answering a follow-up in Tamil, offline.

“Three models, all night, on the NPU. The vapour chamber you bought for gaming is why it doesn’t throttle at hour six.”
4:10
The gap, in thirty seconds.

Real safety AI exists only inside $199–$399 hardware. The free two-phone monitor apps have no intelligence at all. India is the fastest-growing market in Asia Pacific at 8.9% CAGR, and nobody occupies the middle.

4:40
Close on the constraint, not the roadmap.

No cloud, no subscription, no new hardware. An awareness aid, not a medical device — and say that you are careful about that line. Then stop talking.

Two things to have ready and unprompted, because Anchor got asked and had answers: what happens when it’s wrong (false-alarm rate from your own overnight run, and the hysteresis constants you tuned), and who designed this (you architected the severity model and the event fusion; the agent implemented it). Both questions are coming.

Flags

Check these before you write any code

Verify
Are you and Vishal in the same bucket?

Teams must stay within one bucket — students or working professionals, not mixed. Your email is a company domain; if Vishal is still a student, you may not be allowed to team up. This decides your roster, so confirm it with Reskilll before anything else.

Verify
IR blaster access may be blocked.

ConsumerIrManager is the Android API and TRANSMIT_IR the permission, but it is deprecated on Android 12+ and reportedly not enabled in legacy mode on vivo and iQOO builds. Test this in your first hour on the loaner. If it fails, ask iQOO’s engineers on the floor — asking is itself a good move, and worst case the answer is no and you fall back to speaker-only actuation, which still closes the loop.

Verify
Prior-work rules and your city’s date.

Bengaluru has already run, which means the judges’ reference point now includes Tokito. Confirm your city, and confirm what you are allowed to bring pre-built — pre-trained models and static content are normally fine, but state it openly on stage either way. “The research is ours from a published paper; every line of this app was written in 30 hours” is a strong sentence, and a discovered omission is a fatal one.

Fix now
The committed Gemini API key.

Still live in ai.py and sample2.py in a public repo, and in git history even if you delete the line. Revoke it in Google AI Studio today. The hackathon build should hold no cloud key at all — that is a feature, not a chore.

Never say
“98.76% accurate” about the cry classifier.

Any judge who searches this space finds the leakage literature and the “snake oil” press coverage in about four minutes. The number is only safe when you are the one dismantling it. Grep the codebase before you demo: the string “your baby is hungry” must not exist anywhere in the UI.

Do this week
Pre-build everything that isn’t event code.

Onboarding and log screens, micro-lesson cards, the demo cry recordings, the crib polygon preset, the pitch deck, the doll and the towel. Pre-download Gemma 3n and YAMNet. Rehearse the Office Kit pairing on a laptop you own. Most teams arrive with nothing pre-cut; the hours you save are hours on the demo.
