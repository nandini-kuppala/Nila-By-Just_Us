Three Pipelines, Not Eight Features

Everything on your list is buildable, and most of it is buildable on-device. But it is not eight features — it is three pipelines and one log, and reading it that way is the difference between shipping it in 30 hours and shipping half of it. Here is the feasibility of each piece, the datasets to download, the watch answer, and where the hours go.

The shape

Eight features collapse into three pipelines

The medicine checker, the toy checker, “what is my baby doing,” the health Q&A and the tips are one pipeline — an image or a voice question, plus retrieved context, plus the log, into a multimodal model. Build it once, demo it four ways. Same for audio: cry detection and cry classification share one log-mel frontend and differ only in the head on top.

Pipeline C is the one to over-invest in. Point the phone at a medicine strip and get a grounded answer back — that is the exact shape of the project that won Bengaluru.

The one design change I'd push back on

Infant pose estimation is a known-hard problem, not a MediaPipe configuration issue: adult-trained models fail on infants because of body-ratio differences, which is why researchers built a dedicated dataset (SyRIP, 1,700 images) and a domain-adapted model (FiDIP) specifically to fix it. You cannot fine-tune that at a hackathon.

Face detection works on infants where pose does not, and it gives you a better safety semantic anyway. Make the alert “I can’t see your baby’s face” rather than “rollover detected.” It covers rolling prone, a blanket over the face, and the camera being blocked or knocked — one rule, three failure modes, and it never claims to know more than it does. Pair it with frame-difference motion energy so you can distinguish “face hidden and still moving” from “face hidden and gone quiet,” and you have a safety layer you can defend to a judge.

Datasets

Training the detector

First, the shortcut: you may not need to train one at all. YAMNet is a 3.7 MB TFLite model pretrained on AudioSet whose 521 classes already include “Baby cry, infant cry.” Ship that in hour one and you have a working detector before you have written a single training script.

Then improve it the cheap way: freeze YAMNet, take its 1,024-dimension embeddings, and train a two-layer head on your own positives and negatives. That is roughly thirty lines of Keras, trains in under a minute on a laptop CPU, and gives you a defensible “we fine-tuned it on X hours of data” line plus a real precision/recall number for the slide.

Negatives — this is what actually decides whether the demo works

A detector is only as good as what you taught it to reject. Pull from ESC-50’s other 49 classes (vacuum, washing machine, door knock, clock alarm, siren, dog, rooster, rain), UrbanSound8K’s 8,732 urban clips, and MUSAN or DEMAND for speech, music and noise beds to mix in.

The negatives that matter most are not in any dataset

Record thirty minutes yourself, on the iQOO, in the room you will demo in: adult conversation, laughter, applause, a squeaky toy, a phone ringtone, a chair scraping, an AC, a ceiling fan, a TV. Then add adult crying and a cat meowing — the two classic false positives.

This matters because of where you will be standing. A hackathon demo floor is a hall with two hundred people in it. A detector that fires on the judges’ laughter ends your pitch in the first fifteen seconds, and no public dataset will protect you from that. Gate on energy, require a 3-of-5 majority vote before declaring a cry, and rehearse it in the actual noise.

Porting

Your classifier will not survive the move to Android as-is

The 461-feature pipeline in ai.py and sample2.py is pure librosa and scipy — pre_emphasis, a 5th-order Butterworth band-pass, then MFCC + deltas + spectral contrast + chroma + tonnetz + mel, reduced to mean/std/skew/max/min. There is no librosa on Android. Reimplementing all of it in Kotlin so the numbers match closely enough that a Random Forest trained on the Python features still behaves is a multi-day job with silent failure as the reward — a slightly different window function and the model just starts guessing.

Retrain the classifier as a small CNN on log-mel spectrograms, export TFLite, and run it on the NPU. Then the detector and the classifier share one frontend and differ only in the head.

Concretely: keep the same audio and the same labels; drop the 461-d handcrafted vector; compute 64-band log-mel over a 960 ms window; train a 3-block CNN or a MobileNetV3-small head; export to TFLite; run through LiteRT with the Qualcomm AI Engine Direct accelerator. You get real-time inference, a CPU-vs-GPU-vs-NPU latency table for the technical-depth slide, and an architecture you can draw on one line.

Do this before the event — it is training work, not build work, and there is no reason to spend hackathon hours on it. While you are retraining, fix the evaluation too: split first, augment only the training fold. It costs you an hour in the notebook and it means the confidence numbers your UI prints are real. Given you are showing classification, having honest confidence is what keeps that feature defensible rather than fragile.

One rule to carry over from the earlier plan, since you are keeping classification: show the label with its confidence and the context that supports it, and never print a bare “Your baby is hungry.” “Most likely hunger — 68% confident, and it’s been 3h10 since the last feed” costs you nothing, survives any judge who knows this literature, and is genuinely more useful to a parent at 3am.

The wrist

Yes, the watch works — and the cheapest tier is free

T0
Any band the parent already owns

Mi Band, boAt, Noise, Fire-Boltt, Amazfit. Your app posts a normal Android notification; the band vendor’s own companion app is already listening via NotificationListenerService and forwards it over BLE. The wrist buzzes and shows your text. You write no Bluetooth code at all.

0 h
T1
Any Wear OS watch, no watch app

Wear OS bridges phone notifications to the paired watch automatically. Same zero code, plus you can attach actions the parent can tap from the wrist.

0 h
T2
A real Wear OS module

Data Layer API — MessageClient and WearableListenerService. Now you get a distinct vibration pattern per severity, a live cry timer on the watch face, “what’s my baby doing?” from the wrist, and dismiss-from-watch. This is where the demo gets memorable, and it is also the first thing to cut.

5 h
T3
Custom BLE GATT to a cheap band

Every band speaks a proprietary protocol. Gadgetbridge-style reverse engineering is real work and gains you nothing a notification doesn’t already do. Don’t.

skip

The constraint nobody mentions until it fails on stage

BLE reaches roughly ten metres, less through a wall. If the phone is in the nursery and the parent is asleep in another room, the link is marginal exactly when it matters. Wear OS papers over this by routing bridged notifications through Wi-Fi and the cloud when Bluetooth drops — which quietly destroys the offline claim that is your best differentiator.

Don’t fight it. Reframe it, using something that is already true of your market. Co-sleeping is the norm in Indian homes; the baby is usually in the same room. So the watch is not solving range — it is solving noise:

The alert can’t be a sound, because a sound wakes the baby you’re trying to settle, and the partner who finally got to sleep. So it’s a tap on your wrist.

That is a better product argument than range extension, it is honest about the physics, and it makes the watch feel designed-in rather than bolted-on. Keep one failsafe from the earlier severity model: for a genuine safety event, if the watch doesn’t acknowledge within two seconds, the phone alarms out loud on its own. The critical alert must never depend on a radio link.

Practical: the loaner phones come from iQOO, the watch does not. Bring your own — one Wear OS watch for T2, and one cheap band so you can show T0 working on hardware a real parent already owns.

Where Sarvam fits — and where it doesn’t

Sarvam is India’s sovereign-AI stack and the models are genuinely strong for Indic work: Saaras v3 ASR across 23 languages, Bulbul v3 TTS across 11, Sarvam-Translate across 23, Sarvam Vision for 23-language document OCR, and Sarvam-105B for chat. All of it is cloud API.

That is the problem. Your headline claim is that nothing about a baby leaves the room, and the hackathon is explicitly about local and open-source models on the Snapdragon NPU. Routing a parent’s question about their infant through a hosted API contradicts both at once, and a judge will notice.

Recommendation: on-device as the default, Sarvam as an explicit, opt-in quality upgrade. Ship a settings toggle that is off by default and says exactly what it does — “Better regional-language answers. Sends your question to Sarvam’s servers. Never sends audio, video, or your baby’s log.” Then in the pitch you get to say the strongest version of the sentence: “It works in Tamil with the phone in airplane mode. If you want higher-quality Tamil and you’re willing to send a question to an Indian provider, that’s a switch — and it’s off by default.” That reads as a considered privacy architecture rather than a limitation, and it flatters both the NPU story and the India story at once.

Focus

What to spend more on, and what to cut

Spend more here

  1. The actions, not the detection. Detection is a solved primitive you get from YAMNet in an afternoon. Nobody has ever been impressed by a label appearing on a screen. What lands is the phone doing something — playing mum’s recorded voice and then checking whether it worked. That is the loop that only a phone can close, and it is the pattern that won Bengaluru.
  2. Pipeline C, aimed at physical objects. Point the phone at a real medicine strip on the table and get a grounded, sourced, offline answer in Tamil. That is a Tokito-shaped demo: physical object in, real-world understanding out, all on the device. It is the single most demo-able thing on your whole list.
  3. Working in a noisy room. Worth more than any additional feature. Your hardest technical constraint is not the model, it is two hundred people talking.

Cut without regret

  • The Wear OS module (T2). Five hours for something T0 already does for free. Build it only if you hit H23 clean.
  • Pose landmarks. Replaced by face presence — this is a cut that makes the product better, not smaller.
  • Any cloud call. Gemma 3n does vision, audio and 140 languages locally. Reaching for an API costs you your best claim.
  • A browsable tips library. Ten cards triggered by real logged events beat an empty shelf with a search box.
  • The 8-class pathology model. Research slide only. It stays out of the app.

Say this about the toy checker

It is a good feature and a small one, but a photo cannot measure whether something fits through a choke tube. Have the answer name what it checked and what it couldn’t: “Small detachable parts visible — for under-3s the rule of thumb is anything that fits through a toilet-roll tube. I can’t judge size from this photo; check it against one.” That is more useful than a confident yes, and it is the difference between a feature a judge likes and one they poke a hole in.
