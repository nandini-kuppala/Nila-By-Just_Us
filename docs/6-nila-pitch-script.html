Every number, and what it does not say

The statistics you must be able to defend

Each of these is checked against the primary source. The amber note is the overreach a judge could catch you in — read those before you record.

A newborn cries and fusses roughly two hours a day.

126 min at 5–6 weeksN = 7,580 · 57 studies · 17 countriesVermillet et al., Child Development 2022

A second, independent meta-analysis — Wolke, Bilgin & Samara, Journal of Pediatrics 2017, 28 diary studies and 8,690 infants — puts it at 117–133 minutes a day through the first six weeks, falling to 68 minutes by weeks 10–12. Two separate teams, two separate corpora, the same magnitude. That is why you can say “about two hours” without hedging.

CarefulDo not say crying “peaks at six weeks.” Wolke found no statistical evidence for a universal six-week peak, and Vermillet’s modelling puts it nearer four weeks. Say “the first three months” and you are safe.

Of infants brought to an emergency department because of crying, 5.1% had a serious underlying cause.

12 of 237Freedman, Al-Harthy & Thull-FreedmanPediatrics 2009;123(3):841–8

This is the strongest version of the argument available, because the population is already selected for worry — these are parents alarmed enough to attend an emergency department. Nineteen in twenty of them had nothing serious. In the general population the rate is lower still.

CarefulNever phrase it as “5% of crying babies are ill.” The denominator is ED attendances for crying, not all crying. Say the full sentence — it is more impressive anyway.

Mothers lose 62 minutes of sleep a night after a birth. It has not fully recovered six years later.

2,541 women · 2,118 menFathers: 13 minRichter et al., Sleep 2019;42(4)

Population-representative German panel, 2008–2015. Sleep satisfaction and duration both reach their lowest point in the first three months postpartum — mothers drop 1.81 points on a ten-point satisfaction scale — and neither returns to pre-pregnancy levels within six years of a first child. Breastfeeding costs a further 14 minutes.

Careful“An hour a night” is a fair round-down of 62 minutes. Do not round it up to “hours.”

In a randomised trial, 80% of newborns fell asleep within five minutes with white noise, against 25% without.

16/20 vs 5/20Neonates aged 2–7 daysSpencer et al., Arch Dis Child 1990;65:135–7

This is the evidence that the middle rung of the ladder is worth building at all — that a sound, by itself, settles a large fraction of babies. It is a genuine randomised comparison, which is rarer in this literature than you would expect.

CarefulForty babies, in 1990. Say “a small randomised trial,” not “studies show.” If a judge with a clinical background pushes, concede the sample size immediately and pivot to the real point: Nila does not assume it works, it measures whether it worked, in this house, on this baby.

A recorded maternal voice lowers neonatal heart rate and pain scores.

8 RCTs · 584 neonatesMeta-analysis, Medicine 2023

The justification for “your own recorded voice plays first.” Live or recorded both qualify in the pooled trials, which is the part that matters for a phone.

CarefulEvery trial in this meta-analysis is procedural pain in a neonatal unit — heel pricks, not settling at home at 2 a.m. Say “the evidence for a mother’s voice comes from neonatal units” and let it be suggestive. Do not present it as proof it settles a four-month-old in a cot.

Infant crying is the most common trigger for abusive head trauma.

Barr, PNAS 2012Age-incidence curve, Child Abuse Negl 2006

The age-incidence curve of hospitalised shaken-baby cases tracks the normal cry curve. This is the sharpest available answer to “is this a real problem or a convenience app,” and it belongs in the Q&A, not in the film.

CarefulNo percentage exists that you can quote here. State the relationship qualitatively and stop. Do not imply Nila prevents abuse — you have no evidence for that and it is an ugly claim to be caught making.

Pooled prevalence of postpartum depression in India is 23.5%.

62 studies · 21,262 women · 2015–2023Southern India: 22%A second meta puts 2020–24 at 19%

The reason Nila carries a mother-facing side at all — her medicines, her records, her questions — rather than treating her purely as the baby’s operator. Use this if a judge asks why the app is not baby-only.

Over a million baby monitors were exposed by a single extracted key, across 118 countries.

1.1M devicesPlus: 2018 Nest takeover · 2014 default-password livestream site

The offline argument, made by somebody else’s incident report rather than by you. Pair it with the practical version: a cloud monitor is also useless in a hostel, on a train, or anywhere the Wi-Fi drops at 3 a.m.

The number nobody has: what fraction of cries would have settled on their own.

Not established in the literature

No study reports it. Infants shift from signalling to self-soothing from around two months, and by twelve months roughly half resettle without calling a parent (Goodlin-Jones, Burnham, Gaylor & Anders, 2001) — but nothing gives you a per-cry figure. Make that the argument rather than hiding it. Every monitor on the market implicitly answers zero: it wakes you for all of them. Nila is the first one that measures the answer, in your house, on your baby, and shows it to you.

CarefulThis is the line most likely to be challenged, and the one you most want challenged. Do not invent a percentage to fill the gap — the absence is the pitch.

16–20 °C is the recommended room temperature for infant sleep.

Lullaby Trust · NHSFan permitted, not aimed at the baby

The justification for the infrared rung — turning a fan down a step is a comfort action with published guidance behind it, not an invention.

CarefulThe same guidance links overheating to SIDS. Do not go near that in the film. Nila is not a medical device, does not monitor breathing, and must never be described as reducing any risk of death. Say “the room is too warm” and stop.

One breath each

How each feature actually works

For the floor, not the film. If a judge picks a feature, this is the shape of the answer — what it uses, why it is built that way, and the one detail that shows it was measured rather than assumed.

Listening all night · 206 MB · 0.04% of a core

mic 16 kHz UNPROCESSED → log-mel 64 bands, 25/10 ms → 0.96 s patch every 480 ms → CNN, 21,218 params, 38 KB → hysteresis 3-of-5 in @ 0.62, 6-out @ 0.42 → episode

The audio source is deliberate: the usual speech source applies automatic gain control, which flattens the very loudness envelope the ladder later reads. Windows overlap by half so a cry starting mid-window is not split across two weak patches. Delegates are tried NNAPI → GPU → CPU, each with a probe inference before it is trusted. Below −55 dBFS nothing runs at all — in a quiet nursery that is nearly every window, and it is most of why this survives a night on battery.

Guessing why 500 trees · ~1 ms · labelled a guess

episode audio → 456 features → RandomForest, flattened to arrays, walked in Kotlin → “Probably tired · 34%”

This is the classifier from the original Why-is-my-Baby-Crying project, retrained through its own pipeline and exported whole, so it reproduces predict_proba exactly. It is shown as “probably”, with a note that it is a guess from the sound — because on infants it has never heard, it is at chance. See the next section.

Acting, then checking the product

12 s log → 20 s play (your voice, else best-scoring sound, 15% explore) + IR fires once → +35 s re-read envelope → write outcome → 55 s escalate if rising → 100 s ceiling

“Settled” is defined, not felt: a settling trend, or an 18 dB drop from peak. The outcome is written to that sound’s record whether it worked or not, Laplace-smoothed, with a 15% exploration rate so the first thing that happens to work is not played forever. Two attempts maximum. The alert names what was tried first — which is the difference between being woken and being informed.

Reading a medicine strip PP-OCRv4 · ONNX Runtime · no LLM

photo → DBNet rotated boxes → angle classifier → CTC recognition → history → identify → interact → adjudicate

ML Kit reads clean upright text well and returns nothing at all when text is rotated or inverted — no rotated-box detection, no angle classifier. That is missing machinery, not a tuning gap, which is why PP-OCRv4 replaced it. Verified on 25° rotation, upside down, and white-on-silver foil. Fuzzy matching survives OCR damage — 1BUPROFENA00 resolves. There is deliberately no language model in this path: it was wired in, and removed after it invented a reassurance on top of a correct AVOID verdict for a penicillin-allergic mother.

Answering a question 46 sourced entries · 7 guardrails

router (emergency · dose · diagnosis · distress) → field-weighted BM25 + stemming + synonyms + coverage gate → retrieved text with its source → Qwen2.5-0.5B shortens → grounding check or discard

The model shortens; retrieval supplies every fact. Guardrails are code, not prompt instructions: emergency phrases bypass retrieval entirely, dose requests are refused outright — a unit test greps the corpus for dose-shaped strings and fails the build if one appears — and the model is never shown a health record. Scored on 159 real questions across three batteries; the honest figure is 25/41 on first contact with the hardest set.

Watching the cot BlazeFace · frame difference

camera → face presence → motion energy → “can’t see the face” / “unusually still”

Presence and movement only. Not breathing, not vitals, not SIDS — the Owlet enforcement history is the reason that line exists and it is not moving.

Trained on. Detection: Donate-a-Cry positives against ESC-50 and UrbanSound8K negatives — 50 environmental classes including the ones that actually fool a cry detector. The first detector fired on cats in five of eight test clips, so every training negative was scored and the hardest mined back in. Cause: the same 457 Donate-a-Cry clips from 221 infants, whose distribution is the problem — 382 of 457 are hungry, and burping has eight clips across seven infants.

Why the split matters. One infant contributes several clips. Split clips at random and the same baby lands in both halves, so the model learns to recognise the infant, not the reason. Each subject is assigned to exactly one side by greedy iterative stratification before anything else happens — an earlier per-class version was caught by an assertion with four infant UUIDs on both sides. Leakage alone is worth +0.51 AUC on the identical model and the identical data.

Why it is better than the published work. Not a better architecture — a valid protocol. Two unrelated model families, ours and a 461-feature RandomForest from a separate project, fail identically under an honest split. That is not a tuning problem, and no architecture fixes it. The independent August 2026 paper adds the third data point: it reproduces the field’s 97.9% by augmenting before splitting, and the same model measures 0.49 subject-wise. The fix is data collected properly — multiple sessions per infant, labels confirmed by what actually worked — not a bigger model.

And the engineering that follows from it. export_tflite.py generates ModelCard.kt from the evaluation reports, so no accuracy claim in the app can drift away from a measurement. The log-mel frontend exists twice — Python for training, Kotlin for inference — pinned to agree bit-for-bit by a golden-fixture test, because a frontend that disagrees means the model sees a different distribution on the device than it was trained on. 182 tests, 0 failures.

On the floor

What they will ask

“Your cause accuracy is terrible. Why are you showing me this?”

Because it is the real number, and the alternative was to show you a fake one. Every published result on this corpus splits clips instead of infants. We built both arms and measured the difference: half an AUC point, on identical model and data. An independent paper last month reached the same conclusion. The app ships the estimate as a labelled guess, and ships detection — which does work, 0.992 — as a measurement.

“Is this a medical device?”

No, and it is engineered not to become one. It does not monitor breathing, oxygen or vital signs. It never states a medicine dose — there is a unit test that greps the corpus for dose-shaped strings and fails the build if one appears. It never diagnoses. The FDA’s action against Owlet is why that line is where it is.

“Why on-device? Cloud would be easier and more accurate.”

Two reasons, and neither is compliance. One: a monitor that needs Wi-Fi at 3 a.m. is not a monitor. Two: over a million baby monitors were exposed last year by a single extracted key across 118 countries. The app has no API keys because it calls no APIs; cleartext is forbidden outright in the network security config; backup is disabled so health documents cannot leave by a cloud transport; and no monitored audio is ever written to disk.

“What does the iQOO 15 specifically give you?”

Three things. The NPU — the detector tries NNAPI first and falls back only if a probe inference fails, and Settings shows you which unit it landed on. The infrared emitter — no Pixel, Galaxy or iPhone flagship has one, which is why the fan rung only exists on hardware like this. And headroom: the night loop is 206 MB resident, so the optional 547 MB language model and a 1.1 GB vision model both fit without the memory manager killing the service.

“What is not finished?”

The vision model is wired end to end and blocked on a LiteRT-LM runtime that is not published yet. Infrared and Wear OS are implemented and test-covered as far as an emulator allows, which is not far — both need real hardware to confirm. The corpus is English only; the language plumbing exists, the 46 entries need a human translator, not a model.

“Could you sell this tomorrow?”

Not without retraining the detector. ESC-50 supplies its negative examples and is CC BY-NC — non-commercial — so the trained detector inherits that restriction. Retraining on permissively licensed negatives is a day of compute, and we would rather say that than find out later.

Before you record

Six things not to say

Each of these is checkable, and wrong

  • “Crying peaks at six weeks.” The 2017 meta-analysis found no statistical evidence for a universal peak. Say “the first three months.”
  • “5% of crying babies have something seriously wrong.” The denominator is emergency-department attendances for crying, not all crying babies.
  • “Studies show white noise settles 80% of babies.” One randomised trial, forty newborns, 1990. Say “a small randomised trial.”
  • “A mother’s recorded voice calms a crying baby.” The trial evidence is procedural pain in neonatal units. Say where it comes from.
  • Anything touching SIDS, breathing, or oxygen. Not a claim the app makes, not a claim you make on its behalf, not even as an aside about room temperature.
  • Any AUC number for the cause classifier other than 0.444. Including in a passing “well, it gets 95% if you…” — that is the exact sentence the whole beat exists to criticise.
  • Turn airplane mode on before you hit record, not during. It should be in the status bar from the first frame, and you should point at it once.
  • Reset to demo data first (Settings → Reset to demo data) so the colic panel, the timeline and the soother ranking all have a week of history behind them.
  • Record on the phone, not the emulator, if the iQOO is in hand — the self-test reporting NNAPI instead of CPU is worth three seconds of screen time on its own.
  • Do not add music. The first twenty seconds are three statistics in a dark room. Silence carries them.

Description

50 characters minimum≈250 words

Nila is an offline infant-care companion for Android, for parents in the first year — and for the breastfeeding mother, not only the baby.

Every monitor on the market ends at notify. It treats a baby who would have settled in forty seconds exactly like one who needs a parent. Yet of infants taken to an emergency department because of crying, only 5.1% had anything serious (Freedman, Pediatrics 2009), while mothers lose 62 minutes of sleep a night and have not recovered it six years later (Richter, Sleep 2019).

Nila adds two rungs before the alert. A 38 KB quantised CNN detects a cry — 0.992 AUC on infants never heard in training — and a hysteresis state machine opens an episode. At 20 seconds it plays the caregiver's own recorded voice, and fires a taught infrared code to turn the fan down. Thirty-five seconds later it re-reads the loudness envelope and measures whether that sound actually helped, writing the outcome back so the sound that works for this baby plays first next time. Only then does it wake anyone — and the alert names what it already tried.

It also reads a medicine strip with PP-OCRv4 on device, rotated or upside down or on foil, and checks it against the mother's own allergies through five staged agents with no language model in that path. Questions are answered from 46 sourced entries retrieved on the phone; an optional Qwen2.5-0.5B shortens the answer and is never allowed to supply a fact.

Zero network calls. No account, no cloud, no audio ever written to disk. Every model ships inside the APK.
